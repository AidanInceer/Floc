Design approach

The higher-level design guide that sits above the visual language. Visual language owns the concrete house style — the paper-and-biro tokens, the components, the exact type stack. This page owns the reasoning applied inside that style: how a page earns clarity, hierarchy, trust and feel, and the recurring mistakes to avoid.

Distilled from real design-review sessions (YC design reviews with design leads from Stripe, Cursor, Instacart, Webflow) and a UI-fundamentals primer — transcripts in inputs/. Two kinds of guidance are woven together: principles to apply and anti-patterns to avoid.

Precedence. Where a general principle here collides with a decision in the visual language, the house style wins — those collisions are called out inline (⚑). The most common ones: we are light-only (no dark mode), and we deliberately run a serif + monospace + hand type system rather than a single sans-serif.

The four questions

Almost every review failure was a page that made a visitor work to answer one of these. Answer them fast, above the fold, in this order of priority:

  1. What is this? — plainly, in the user's words.
  2. Is it for me? — who it's for, made obvious.
  3. Does it work? — show the product; proof it does the job.
  4. Is it credible? — social proof, last, not first.

Clarity & messaging

The single most common failure across every review was not knowing what the product does. Optimise the words before anything else.

  • Say what it is literally. A clever headline is fine only if a plain sub-headline underneath states the actual thing and who it's for. If a headline can't stand alone, it's failing.
  • Use the user's own words. Ask real users to describe the product and lift their phrasing. Avoid invented internal vocabulary ("progressive discovery") and abstract slogans ("instant sustainability with automated storytelling").
  • Don't define yourself by other products ("X for email", "the power of ChatGPT"). Own it and explain it.
  • Benefits and problems over features. "Generate custom API responses" is a feature; nobody cares until you frame the pain it removes.
  • Pass the headline-skim test. People scroll fast and read only headlines. Read just your headlines top to bottom — do they tell the whole story? Don't bury the message in body copy.
  • Don't emphasise the negative. Skimmers read bold and headline words; bolding "trial and error" or opening with "stop doing X" plants the wrong takeaway. ⚑ For us this reinforces sentence-case copy that says what happened ("Priya pays Tom £42.50"), not app-speak.
  • One name. Two names for one thing is pure friction.
  • Trust the scroll; unveil in layers. Don't cram every edge-case and CTA above the fold — peel the onion, one idea per scroll.

Visual hierarchy

Hierarchy is created by contrast — the gap between big and small, colour and neutral, top and bottom. A layout with no contrast reads like a spreadsheet.

  • Three levers: size, position, colour. The most important thing is largest, highest, and most distinct. Secondary detail (dates, labels) goes smaller and below.
  • One main call-to-action per scroll. Every screen should have a single clear priority; competing buttons and "join our Discord / GitHub / sign up / book a demo" all at once kills it. Make the primary action obvious and demote the rest to secondary links.
  • Every pixel should earn its place. Redundant lines, noise headings ("trusted by the best"), and decorative dividers add clutter without value — cut them.
  • Draw relationships, don't just label them. An icon + line from A → B beats the words "from A to B".

Layout, spacing & grids

  • Whitespace over grid dogma. Letting things breathe matters more than forcing everything onto a 12-column grid. Grids earn their keep on repeating/structured content (galleries, lists) and for responsive behaviour.
  • Group what belongs together. Proximity is hierarchy — tighter spacing between related items, more space between groups.
  • Consistent spacing scale. A 4-point base (everything a multiple) keeps rhythm consistent because values always halve cleanly. ⚑ Our radii (3/5/6px) and spacing already follow this restraint.
  • Design for small screens. Always check tablet and phone — many users arrive on mobile.

Typography

  • Tighten large text. On big headings, pull letter-spacing to about −2/−3% and line-height to ~110–120%; loose display text reads amateur.
  • Limit sizes. ≤6 font sizes for a landing page (wide range); far fewer and smaller for dense/dashboard screens.
  • ⚑ House exception: the general advice is "one sans-serif, everywhere". We deliberately run three faces with strict roles — --serif for prose/headings, --type (mono, tabular) for every figure/date/label and all money, --hand for marginalia only. See visual language → legibility rule. Don't collapse this to one font.

Colour

  • Colour for purpose, not decoration. Use semantic colour that carries meaning: blue = trust/action, red = danger, yellow = warning, green = success. Let colour signify.
  • Start from one primary and ramp it (lighten for backgrounds, darken for text) rather than scattering hues.
  • ⚑ House palette: ours is ink + highlighter + biro-blue (pen), with a fixed three-state status meaning (agreed / open / needs action) applied identically everywhere, and never colour alone — every status also carries a word. See visual language → tokens.
  • ⚑ No dark mode. The general "designing in dark mode" guidance does not apply — the whole metaphor is ink on paper; a dark notebook is a different product. Light-only is a decision, not an omission.

Affordances & signifiers

Good UI signifies how it works so you don't have to write instructions. A container groups related things; a highlighted item reads as selected; greyed text reads as inactive. Make interactive things look interactive.

  • Give buttons a press state, nav items an active highlight, controls hover states, and unclear controls a tooltip. A mystery toggle with no tooltip is a reviewed anti-pattern — nobody knows what it does.
  • Don't rely on the user guessing that something scrolls, plays, or is clickable — an unlabelled slider or a play target with no play button gets missed. If you must tell users to "scroll to explore", it isn't intuitive.

Feedback & states

Every interaction needs a response. Design the states, not just the happy path.

  • Buttons: default, hover, active/pressed, disabled — plus a loading state where the action takes time.
  • Inputs: focus, error (red border + message), sometimes warning. ⚑ But don't yell too early — validate when the user is done, not on every keystroke or a trailing space (the Stripe email-validation fix).
  • Async: show real progress. Don't scatter spinners in empty states or loop unhelpful "thinking…" text; surface actual states/steps and focus attention. ⚑ Our tab loading already renders a blank ruled page under a pending tab.

Motion & micro-interactions

  • Never hijack the scroll. Unanimously hated across every reviewer. Don't take over the scroll to force a sequence.
  • Motion steals the eye — aim it deliberately. Animate the one thing you want noticed; movement while the user is reading, or a load-in animation that pulls focus off the headline, is a net negative.
  • Micro-interactions confirm actions (a "copied" chip sliding up). Practical first, playful second.
  • Avoid meaningless motion — blob backgrounds and fast auto-loops that say nothing about the product. ⚑ Our motion budget is colour transitions only, and nothing under prefers-reduced-motion.

Shadows & depth

  • Subtle wins. Most shadows are too strong — lower opacity, raise blur. Cards need very little; only floating content (popovers) needs more. If the shadow is the first thing you notice, it's wrong.
  • Massive drop shadows are an AI-slop tell (see below). ⚑ Our cards carry no shadow at all; the page's paper texture supplies depth.

Imagery, screenshots & demos

  • Show the product, but show it legibly. Screenshots that are too small or too far away convey nothing — zoom into the specific, meaningful part. Text-heavy screenshots (code, logs) fight the copy; abstract or shrink them.
  • A video thumbnail must add value unclicked — many won't press play. A video whose still is just a screenshot already shown adds nothing.
  • Prefer a live try over a hero reel (a text box, a few free actions) — and pick the demo carefully: a bigger demo that exposes flaws can unsell.
  • Handle people-photos with care. Don't doctor headshots intrusively; keep styles consistent; keep them current.
  • Overlays: a linear gradient (optionally a progressive blur) keeps an image readable under text far better than a flat full-screen scrim.

Avoiding the "vibe-coded" look

Reviewers spotted AI-generated sites instantly. The tells:

  • Purple gradients, oversized shadows, generic default icon sets, weak typography — the styles AI reaches for by default.
  • Details that "don't melt together": screenshots at different sizes, some shadowed some not, borders around borders, boxes inside boxes.
  • No continuity — marketing page, app and pricing each looking like a different product.
  • A generic, unreadable, or off-message logo; the company name nowhere findable.

The fix is intentionality: a small, robust set of tokens and components, used consistently, with every choice made on purpose. That is exactly what the visual language exists to enforce — this is the case for taking it seriously.

The underlying discipline

Why any of this holds up over time (from Stripe's head of design):

  • The gravitational pull is to mediocrity. Quality is the sum of daily micro-decisions; details slip by default unless actively resisted.
  • Quality checklist, in order: functional → usable → craft & beauty. Craft isn't optional; defects make people doubt the whole thing works, and small slips (typos, inconsistent casing) quietly erode trust — "if you missed that detail, what else did you miss?"
  • Regressions are inevitable. A perfect flow degrades where it meets newer features (redo the dining room and the kitchen looks shabby). Re-walk the whole journey, not just the new screen.
  • You can't judge it from inside. Get real users thinking aloud, continuously — and remember the problem is often the step before the page you're staring at.
