How Floc could make money

The options, and the ones ruled out. Nothing here is a commitment.

The first slice is decided and built: see Pro tier. This page stays as the wider map, and above all as the rejected list that slice must not quietly cross.

The constraint that shapes everything

The pitch is a shared notebook the group trusts. Trust is the asset, and most travel revenue models spend it:

  • Booking commission means the recommendation is bought. The moment a hotel appears because it pays, the group stops believing the route.
  • Advertising means selling the group's attention while they're trying to make a decision — the one moment they came for.
  • Data resale ends the product. A trip is who you travel with, when your home is empty, and what you can afford.
  • Taking a cut of settlements turns the ledger into a payment rail, which is regulated, expensive, and would make the money tab feel like a toll booth.

So: the group's core planning is free forever, and nothing that touches their money costs money. Every option below has to survive that.

The shape of the market

The software is used hard for six weeks, then not at all for eleven months. Subscriptions fit badly. What people will pay for is either something at the moment of peak value, or a use case that isn't "seven friends, one trip a year". Three buckets follow.

Bucket 1 — Charge at the moment of peak value

Not while deciding — while travelling. Willingness to pay is real there, and what is sold isn't a feature the group needed to plan with. The end-of-trip keepsake that used to sit here is now rejected.

The offline/travel pack

A one-off — £3–5 per trip, bought by anyone, unlocked for the group — bundling what matters while you are away: a real offline copy, printable day sheets, a wallet card with the route and everyone's numbers. Priced like a guidebook. It doesn't degrade the free tier, because the free tier is about deciding.

Bucket 2 — Charge the groups that aren't the core case

Six to nine friends, one or two trips a year, stays free forever. Some groups look like that and use the product ten times harder.

Very large groups

A 40-person trip is a different product with different problems (sub-groups, who's in which house, coach seats). Charging above some member threshold is defensible, but the number has to sit well clear of a normal friend group — 25+, not 10 — or it reads as a bait-and-switch on the core promise.

Bucket 3 — Charge for depth, not for access

The classic freemium line, drawn so that no trip is ever crippled:

Free forever Paid ("the archive")
Unlimited trips, unlimited members —
Every planning surface, the full ledger —
Your last N trips, in full Every trip you've ever taken, forever
Export everything, always Search across all trips at once
— Attachments/receipt photos beyond a storage cap

Honest, because what is charged for is storage over time — the thing that genuinely costs money. But it only earns from heavy long-term users. A supplement, not a business. Any price here has to sit against Pro's £29.99/year rather than beside it; two subscriptions is one too many.

Explicitly rejected

Idea Why not
Booking commission / affiliate hotel links Buys the recommendation; kills the trust the product runs on. If a search link is ever added it must be a plain deep link that pays nothing.
Advertising Selling the group's attention during the decision they came to make.
Selling or brokering trip data Ends the product. Not a pricing question.
A cut of settlements Turns the ledger into a payment rail: regulated, expensive, and it makes the honest bit feel like a toll.
Charging per trip member Punishes exactly the behaviour the product needs (inviting the ninth person).
The printed book / paid PDF export A physical-goods business bolted onto software — print, shipping, returns, customer service — for a thin margin on a once-a-year moment. The keepsake is the product's emotional payoff; charging for it taxes the one thing groups come back for.
Organiser plans (templates, rosters, cross-trip money, own branding) Was the strongest recurring-revenue candidate on paper, but it is a second product with a second audience — people running trips for others, not with them. Building it means splitting focus before the core case has demand. If it returns, it returns as its own thing, not as a Pro tier.
Paywalling the money tab It's the most-loved feature and the one with the clearest alternative (a spreadsheet). Charging for it loses users, not converts them.

What actually happened

The original plan was: ship v1 free, watch what groups reach for, then price one thing. Steps 1 and 2 were skipped. A subscription was built first, on test keys, to understand the plumbing while the commercial questions stay open — see Pro tier. Analytics is out of scope for v1, so "instrument the paying moments" has no mechanism behind it and is not scheduled.

Two rules survive intact:

  1. Build nothing that makes the options above impossible.
  2. Never revisit the rejected list to close a revenue gap. The gap is a signal about the product, not about the pricing.

Open questions

  • Is per-trip one-off pricing (the travel pack) more natural than a subscription for a product used in bursts? Probably — but it caps revenue hard, and Pro is now the shipped shape.
  • What's the minimum storage cost per archived trip? That number decides whether Bucket 3 is honest or arbitrary.
