Waypoint Pro — the decided shape

Unlike the monetisation page, which is options, this page is decisions. It records what Pro is, who it belongs to, and how the gate works, together with the reasoning — so a future revisit argues with the argument rather than rediscovering it.

The honest framing: no revenue is expected for a long time. Nothing is advertised and nobody is being asked to buy. The point of building this now is that the plumbing exists, is understood, and is shaped so the commercial questions stay open.

Legal position (UK)

  • No limited company is needed. Trading as a sole trader is lawful from the first sale. Register for HMRC Self Assessment once trading income passes £1,000 in a tax year.
  • VAT is irrelevant below the £90,000 UK threshold.
  • EU VAT has no threshold. A digital subscription sold to an EU consumer triggers VAT from the first euro, meaning VAT OSS registration. This is the single biggest piece of overhead available to avoid, and the reason sales are GB-only (see below).
  • Before a real payment is taken, the site must publish: terms, a privacy policy, a cancellation/refund policy covering the 14-day right to cancel digital purchases (waivable if the buyer consents to immediate access), and a contact address. Taking money without these is the actual exposure — not the trading.

Consequence: going live is not a code change. It is a ticket whose content is legal pages, Stripe account activation, and one environment variable.

Payment platform

Stripe, restricted to GB. Checkout is created with an allowed-countries list of GB alone.

Shopify is a storefront for physical goods and was never the right tool. A merchant of record is the right answer eventually — group travel is international and GB-only is a real ceiling — but "eventually" means after revenue exists. Swapping is cheap while entitlement is one function and one table. Stripe wins now on fees, on test-mode quality, and on being the best surface to learn the mechanics on.

Pricing

  • £3.99/month or £29.99/year.
  • No free trial. A trial is a conversion lever and there is nothing to convert. Adding one later is a Stripe setting plus trialing in the status check.

An earlier £20/year was rejected: against £3.99/month it is 58% off, so nobody would ever choose monthly, and it teaches buyers the monthly price is fiction. £29.99 is a discount a buyer recognises as real. If £20 is wanted, it should run as a launch offer with an end date — same money, no permanent anchor.

Rule: prices live in Stripe as Price objects; the app stores only Price IDs, in environment variables. No amount is ever hardcoded, so a price change is a dashboard action rather than a deploy.

Who owns Pro

A trip is Pro if any of its members is Pro. Uncapped.

The alternative considered was a seat ratio — one Pro member covers four people, a fifth member requires a second payer. Analogues exist (Spotify Family, Nintendo Switch Online, Costco member-plus-guest), but every one of them sells a fixed number of seats to one buyer. The ratio inverts that: the group must contain enough independent payers or the trip degrades. Rejected for three reasons:

  1. It is charging per trip member wearing a disguise — already on the rejected list on the monetisation page, because it punishes the ninth invite, which is exactly the behaviour the product needs.
  2. It flickers. Trip capability would depend on a live count of other people's billing, so a lapsed card silently removes features mid-trip.
  3. It is socially unenforceable. It asks friends to police each other's subscriptions — the awkward money conversation Waypoint exists to remove.

At zero users, a nine-person trip where eight people see Pro working for free is not lost revenue; it is the only funnel there is. Extraction is a problem for a product with demand.

The guard against changing minds is structural, not documentary: the decision lives in one function, canUseProFeature() in server/entitlements.ts. Seat caps, ratios, or personal-only Pro all become a policy edit inside that function rather than a refactor.

What Pro gates

Deliberately a small, boring set. The purpose of the first cut is to prove the pipe, not to make Pro attractive.

  • Weather — built (server/weather.ts).
  • The automatic packing planner — built (server/packing-generator.ts).
  • Multiple notes pages — not built. Today server/note-doc.ts is one row, one blob, per trip. A later ticket.

The organiser features from the monetisation page (templates, rosters, cross-trip money) remain the strongest long-term bet, but building a Pro feature and the billing that gates it at the same time is two hard things at once. They arrive later as additions to a tier that already exists.

How a lock looks

Visible, honest, inert. The surface stays where it is and states plainly that it is a Pro feature. No blurred sample, no fake forecast, no nag. Blurred placeholder data is a small lie told to a friend group who will screenshot it at each other, and this product's only asset is being believed.

What happens when a subscription lapses

Read-only. Never hidden, never deleted. A generated packing list and any Pro-created content stay visible forever; what stops is the action — regenerating, adding another page, refreshing the forecast.

This is the one rule that protects the core promise: content vanishing mid-trip because someone's card expired would end the product's claim to be a notebook the group can trust.

It also simplifies the architecture sharply: the gate is only ever consulted on writes and refreshes, never on reads. Most read paths never touch billing at all.

Architecture

Where the truth lives

A local subscription table in Turso, written by Stripe webhooks (checkout.session.completed, customer.subscription.updated, customer.subscription.deleted). Roughly: userId, stripeCustomerId, stripeSubscriptionId, status, currentPeriodEnd, cancelAtPeriodEnd.

Querying Stripe per request was rejected: it puts a network call on every page load and makes Waypoint unavailable whenever Stripe is. A plain user.isPro boolean was rejected because it cannot express "cancelled, but paid up until 14 November" — which is what actually happens when someone cancels.

Two non-negotiables that come with webhooks: the signature is verified (otherwise anyone can POST themselves a subscription), and every write is idempotent (Stripe retries, and a retry must be harmless).

The gate

One function, one file. canUseProFeature(tripId) asks: does any non-deleted member of this trip hold a subscription row whose status is active or trialing, with currentPeriodEnd in the future?

Enforced on the server. The client never decides. Hiding a tab is presentation; the weather read refusing is the gate.

Hand-rolled, not the Better Auth plugin

@better-auth/stripe would generate the checkout, portal, webhook and table in fewer lines. Rejected on repo-specific grounds:

  • pnpm verify runs a migration-drift check; a plugin owning tables outside schema.ts is a fight with that check rather than with Stripe.
  • depcruise and knip police layering, and injected routes and schema sit badly with both.
  • Understanding the plumbing is an explicit goal here, and the plugin's whole value is hiding it.

Cost: roughly 200 lines and an extra afternoon.

Keys and environments

Test keys everywhere, including production, until the legal pages exist. The Buy button works, the webhook fires, no money moves. develop is always test. Going live is a one-variable change in Railway, gated on its own ticket.

Open questions

  • GB-only is a real ceiling for a product about international travel. What volume justifies moving to a merchant of record?
  • Weather and the packing planner shipped free. Gating them now costs nothing because there are no users — but it sets the precedent that a shipped feature can move behind the paywall. Is that a precedent worth setting?
  • Uncapped trip inheritance means one payer can serve an arbitrarily large group. What actual usage pattern would make that worth revisiting?
